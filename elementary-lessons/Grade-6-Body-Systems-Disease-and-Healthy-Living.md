# Grade 6 Lessons — The Human Body in Health: Systems, Disease and Healthy Living (Health Science, Elementary)

**Level:** Grade 6 (Elementary, ≈ ages 11–12)
**Strand:** Health · taught inside **General Science**, here planned as its own unit
**Unit:** Grade 6, Period IV (Semester Two), with Week 1 drawing on Period II ·
**Duration:** 4 weeks · 40-minute periods
**Source:** *Liberia National Curriculum — General Science, Grades 1–6*, Ministry of
Education (`uploads/General Science1-6.pdf`): **Guide pp. 70–73** “THE HUMAN BODY ·
HEALTH LESSONS” and **Guide pp. 66–67** (ecosystem and the HIV-prevention activity).
The guide names its own text for this unit: Darsaw Arthur & Fischer-Buder Karin,
*New Elementary Science for Liberia, Grade 6 Pupil's Book*, Pearson 2014.
(“Guide p. N” = the printed guide page.)

---

## How to teach this unit in Grade 6

- **This is the year the body becomes a system.** Not “the heart is for pumping”
  but *how the blood goes round, and what happens when it cannot.*
- **Measure, then argue.** The pulse investigation supplies the numbers every
  lesson refers back to: “*your pulse went up because the muscle needed more
  oxygen, and the blood is how the oxygen travels.*”
- **Discussion with facts on the table.** The risky-behaviour and HIV lessons work
  only when the guide's own facts (transmission, non-transmission, the stages,
  testing and treatment) are on the wall before the talk starts.
- **Grade 6 meets the guide's sensitive line honestly:** the guide prints its own
  instruction to pay attention to proprietary information; the practice here is
  same-sex small groups for the menstrual talk, no personal disclosure in the
  open class, and a parent notice beforehand.
- **Assess the writing.** A brochure, a plan and a report — three pieces of real
  writing for the folio, since Grade 6 must leave primary school able to write
  about health, not only recite it.

## Before you begin — Safeguarding

1. **Charts and correct words, clothes on.** The reproductive organs are named on
   the diagram; no pupil demonstrates anything on a body.
2. **Never ask who is sexually active, who is in love, or who is sick.** The
   “risky behaviour” work uses invented case cards only.
3. **No name-calling about bodies or orientation** — and the guide makes
   discrimination itself a health topic (p. 72: *it can cause depression, fear,
   poor self-esteem, poor school work and even death*).
4. **A disclosure is listened to once, written in the child's words, and reported
   the same day** to the head teacher and the carer; in Liberia to the police
   **Women and Children Protection Unit** and the **Ministry of Gender, Children and
   Social Protection**. Recent assault goes to a health facility at once.
5. **Consent is taught as a rule, not a suggestion:** *no one may touch you or
   show you anything sexual without your agreement, and your agreement can be
   taken back.* A pupil who repeats sexual talk about a classmate is corrected as
   a safeguarding matter, not as mischief.

# THE MODEL LESSON — “Rest and go: what your pulse tells you”

**Where it sits:** Week 1, Period 3 — the investigation that gives the unit its
numbers. Guide pp. 70–71 (circulatory and respiratory systems; the guide's own
activity “*Investigate the pulse rate of your friends before and after exercise.*”).
**Class:** Grade 6, 40 minutes.

**Objectives.** By the end of the period each pupil can —
1. find their own pulse at the wrist or neck and count it accurately for one minute (or 15 beats × 4);
2. state the **circulatory** system's work and name the three kinds of blood vessel;
3. explain in one sentence why the pulse and the breathing both rise after exercise;
4. read the class results table and name the highest and lowest after-exercise rate, and one reason for a difference.

**Materials.** A watch or clock with a second hand (or a stopwatch) for every
pair · a ball or skipping rope · the circulatory chart or torso model · chart paper
for the class table · the pupils' science journals · a ruler.

**Before the lesson:** run the whole class once through *how* to take a pulse, so
that nobody spends the period hunting for a beat. **Find the pulse on the inside
of the wrist, just below the thumb.** Press with two fingers, not the thumb, and do
not press hard. Count for a full minute the first time.

### Stage 1 — Introduction (5 minutes)

1. Put the chart on the board: **heart · blood · vessels.** Ask: *which system
   carries food and oxygen to every part of the body?* (the circulatory system —
   guide p. 70: “the heart pumps blood in arteries, veins and capillaries”).
2. “Today we do not read about it. We **measure** it.”
3. Quick demonstration: the teacher takes their own pulse, counts aloud, writes the
   number. “Your heart beats without you telling it. It is a muscle.”

### Stage 2 — Main activities (25 minutes)

1. **Skill first (4 min).** Everyone finds their own pulse; teacher walks the rows,
   fixes finger position. One practice count of 15 seconds × 4.
2. **Resting rate (5 min).** Sitting quietly, eyes on the clock. **Two** counts,
   and take the nearer average. Write in the journal: *resting = ___ beats per
   minute.* No talking during the count — talking raises the pulse.
3. **The exercise (4 min).** Two minutes of the same work for every pair:
   star jumps, or skipping, or running on the spot — **the same** for the whole
   class, or the numbers cannot be compared. Immediately: find, count, record
   *after exercise = ___*. Then, one minute later: *after 1 minute of rest = ___*.
4. **The class table (6 min).** One pupil writes every pair's three numbers on the
   chart; the class reads it. Find together: the **lowest** resting rate, the
   **highest** after-exercise rate, and the **biggest rise**. Ask the guide's own
   question, and let pupils answer it:
   “*Why do your heart and your breathing both speed up when you run?*”
   → *the muscles need more oxygen and must get rid of more carbon dioxide; the
   lungs take in more air and the heart pumps faster to carry it.* (Circulatory and
   respiratory systems **working together** — objective 2.)
5. **Name the vessels (4 min).** On the chart, label: **arteries** (blood driven
   away from the heart), **veins** (blood returning), **capillaries** (tiny — where
   the exchange with the cells happens). Ask where the pulse can be felt and
   why. Then: “*which of the three would you expect to see a pulse in?*”
6. **Fair test, in one line (2 min).** “*Why must everyone do the same exercise,
   for the same time, and be counted the same way?*” — a fair test.

### Stage 3 — Summary (5 minutes)

- The sentence the class dictates into the journal, with the numbers: “*My heart
  rose from ___ to ___ because my muscles needed more oxygen and had to get rid of
  carbon dioxide.*”
- Two answers, both correct: “*Some pupils rose more than others — why?*”
  (age, size, how fit, whether they had eaten, whether they walked here, and
  counting error). Emphasise: **a difference is not a mistake**, but we must be
  able to explain it.
- The health link: “*How many hours of sleep should a child your age have?*” —
  the guide's own figure: **8–10 hours** (p. 72). Exercise, rest and a balanced
  diet are what keep this system strong.

### Stage 4 — Evaluation (5 minutes)

| Ask | Expected answer |
|---|---|
| Name the three kinds of blood vessel and one thing each does. | arteries carry blood away from the heart; veins carry blood back to the heart; capillaries are tiny vessels where gases and food pass to and from the cells |
| What is the heart's work? | it is a muscle that pumps blood round the body through the vessels |
| Why does the pulse go up during exercise? | the muscles need more oxygen and must remove more carbon dioxide, so the heart beats faster to deliver and remove them |
| Why is a fit person's resting pulse often lower? | a stronger heart pushes more blood at each beat, so it needs fewer beats |
| Which two systems are working together when you run? | the respiratory (lungs) and the circulatory (heart and blood) systems |
| **Written, one sentence each:** what is the circulatory system's function? What did your numbers show? | any correct statement of pumping and transport; a report giving their own two numbers and the rise |

**Model answers for the teacher** are the right-hand column above: a wrong answer
is re-taught with the chart or the demonstration — in a health lesson a wrong
answer is a habit not yet formed, not a mark to lose.

### Assignment and home practice

- Take your resting pulse when you wake tomorrow, before you get up. Record it
  beside yesterday's number. Then walk to the far end of the compound, count again,
  and write **one sentence** on what changed and why.
- Ask one adult at home what they do for exercise and how many hours they sleep.
  Bring both answers.
- Journal: draw the heart and label one artery, one vein and one capillary.

### Common mistakes, and how to correct them

| You will see | Say / do |
|---|---|
| counting with the thumb (it has its own pulse) | two flat fingers on the wrist, press gently, not the thumb |
| counting for 10 seconds and multiplying by 6 | use 15 × 4 for a slow pulse; the shorter the count, the bigger the error |
| talking and laughing during the count | counting is silent; the partner holds the watch and calls “stop” |
| every pupil ran a different exercise | set one exercise for the whole class before you start, or the table is meaningless |
| “my heart is in my left side” | the heart sits in the **middle** of the chest, a little to the left; felt on the left because it beats towards the wall |
| a pupil frightened by a fast number | normal: after exercise a pulse of 120–160 in your age is expected and falls back within minutes |
| “exercise is bad for the heart” | the heart is a muscle; exercise trains it — that is why a fit person's resting pulse is lower |

### Differentiation (the guide's “inclusive and differentiated learning”)

- **Support:** count 15 seconds with a partner holding the watch; use a
  prepared table with the numbers already ruled; let the pupil report orally.
- **Extend:** pupils compute the **rise** (after minus resting) for every pair,
  find the class average, and answer “*whose recovery was quickest, and what does
  that tell you about them?*” They may also graph the class table.
- **A pupil who cannot run:** they do the arm-and-leg work seated, or they are the
  timekeeper and recorder — both are real science roles and both belong in the
  table.
- **Anxiety about bodies:** nobody reads their numbers aloud; only the pair tells
  the scribe, and the scribe writes initials only.

👀 **Look for by Friday (most of the class):** finds and counts a pulse correctly;
records three numbers and explains the rise; names the three vessel types; links
breathing and pulse; writes the sentence with their own figures.

   fingers up: “**A**bstain, **B**e faithful, **C**ondom — and know your status.”

   first.*”
